# Serial Suspense Playbook

How we write multi-part reels that people watch to the last second and follow for the next part.

The workflow (plan, then folders, then scripts) lives in `.claude/skills/story-series/SKILL.md`. This file holds the craft. The evidence behind it is in `research/competitor-reels/ANALYSIS.md`.

---

## 0. The three numbers we write for

| Number | Question | What decides it |
|---|---|---|
| **Hold** (0–3s) | Did they stop scrolling? | The first frame, the first spoken line, and the on-screen text, all delivering the same hook |
| **Watch-through** | Did they reach the last line? | A turn every 10–15s, false relief before the final drop, and the best line saved for last |
| **Pull** | Did they follow, comment, or go looking for the next part? | The strength of the series question, the cliffhanger, the CTA, and how easy it is to find Part 1 |

Every line must serve one of these. If a line serves none of them, cut it.

## 1. What the reference pages teach (summary)

- **@saveo.house** (Every Dollar Leaves a Trail): "you" POV, real facts as obstacles, a Suspicion % meter, numbered rules, and "comment X or Y, the majority writes the next part". 180K–900K likes per part, and the comment rate *grows* each episode (1.3% → 6.3%).
- **@beanie.escape** (Apartment 304): first-person horror with ordinary-looking menace and endings where the villain is right behind you. 67K → 19K → 9K likes, so it loses most of its audience after Part 1. It also leaves a cliffhanger unpaid and has weak navigation.
- **Our formula:** beanie's dread and character menace, plus saveo's participation mechanics and fact layer, minus beanie's leaks.

Both pages run **87–107s per part**, at **~170 words per minute**, which is **about 250–290 words**.

## 2. Choose the story engine

| Engine | POV | What the viewer is asking | How each part ends | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Survival horror** | "I", past tense | "Will they get out?" | A reveal, a threat, or the villain right behind them | beanie.escape |
| **B. Interactive dilemma** | "You", present tense | "What would I do?" | A binary vote | saveo.house |
| **C. Mystery** | "I" or "you" | "Who or what is behind it?" | A new clue pointing somewhere worse | |
| **D. Countdown** | Either | "Will they make it in time?" | The clock jumps, or time is stolen | |
| **E. Reality what-if** | "You" | "What would *really* happen if…?" | A consequence spiral driven by real facts | saveo.house |

**Hybrids win.** Pair one emotional engine (A, C, or D) with a participation engine (B) and, where it fits, a fact layer (E). For example: "A + B" is a horror story where viewers vote on the hero's moves, and "C + E" is a mystery solved with real forensic facts.

## 3. The premise

**Formula:** [ordinary person] + [one wrong detail, an irresistible offer, or something already done] + [a reason they can't just leave] + [a clock]

### Premise generators
1. **Too good to be true:** $600 rent for a penthouse; $5,000 for one night shift; a free house from a relative you never met.
2. **The rules:** "Night-shift rule 1: never answer the phone in room 9."
3. **You already did it:** "You just stole $10M / found a body in your trunk / won the lottery and someone saw the ticket."
4. **The wrong detail:** "My daughter's imaginary friend drew our address. And our *new* address."
5. **The message from nowhere:** texts from your own number; a voicemail dated tomorrow.
6. **The found object:** a phone full of recordings, a key with a room number, a diary describing *your* day.
7. **Survival what-if:** "You wake up on a plane and everyone else is asleep." "You fall overboard 5 miles from shore."
8. **The isolated place:** a lighthouse, a night-shift gas station, a research station, a cruise ship, a remote cabin.
9. **The trusted person who isn't:** a helpful neighbor, a new partner, the cop who takes your statement.
10. **The real stat:** a startling true number as the hook, then a story built on it.

### Premise tests (it must pass all 5)
1. **One breath:** it can be said in one sentence of ≤ 20 words.
2. **"Why not just leave or call the police?"** has a strong, built-in answer.
3. **Hidden truth:** the writer can state the secret in one sentence *before* writing Part 1.
4. **Room to escalate:** you can list at least as many bigger dangers as there are parts.
5. **Comment test:** viewers will have a theory, an opinion, or a choice to post.

## 4. Series architecture

### 4.1 Series question
One question that only the final part answers ("What happens to the new tenant on Thursday?", "Do you get away with it?"). **Each part answers a small question and opens a bigger one.**

### 4.2 Hidden truth first
Write down what is *really* going on before any script. Every clue points to it. This is what keeps a series from wandering and dropping threads, as beanie did with its top-floor cliffhanger.

### 4.3 Loop ledger (the most important tool)
A loop is an open question in the viewer's head.
- Keep **2–4 loops open** at all times. With fewer, there's no reason to come back; with more, viewers get confused.
- Every part **closes at least 1 loop and opens at least 1 bigger one**. The finale closes them all.
- **Every cliffhanger is paid off within the first 20 seconds of the next part.**
- Only one loop may stay open after the finale, as the deliberate Season 2 door.

### 4.4 Escalation ladder
Stakes rise in *kind*, not just in amount:
discomfort → the plan or property is in danger → physical danger → someone else is in danger → identity or reality shifts ("I was the previous tenant") → nowhere is safe.
**Every part must end in a worse place than it started.** If it doesn't, rewrite it.

### 4.5 Cast
- At most **4 named characters** in a 3–5-part series, and at most 6 in a 10-part one.
- **At most one new named character per part.**
- Each character gets a *surface*, a *secret*, and a *signature line or tic* (the old woman's "sweetheart", Dre's "$400 in his account").

### 4.6 Recurring devices
These make the series recognizable in one frame:
- a number or place (Apartment 304)
- a time (3 a.m.)
- a meter (Suspicion %, Days Left, Oxygen %, Battery %)
- numbered rules
- a repeated phrase that gains a new meaning later

### 4.7 Plants and payoffs
Every major reveal is planted at least one part earlier, in plain sight. When a viewer rewatches an earlier part, they should spot the clue. That drives profile binges and rewatches.
A short-range plant can pay off within the same part: saveo's "That should worry you" is paid off 25 seconds later by Dre's text.

### 4.8 Twist rules
A good twist is **fair** (it was planted), **recontextualizing** (it changes the meaning of earlier scenes), **surprising in the moment**, and **inevitable in hindsight**. The strongest flips are:
- the trusted person is the threat
- the hero's assumption about themselves is wrong
- the place or time isn't what we thought

### 4.9 Finale
The finale answers the series question, pays off the biggest plant, and lands an emotional button: justice, horror, or irony. An optional final sting opens Season 2.

## 5. Blueprints by number of parts

Each part must still work for a stranger (§6, Stranger test).

### 3 parts (tight, ~4.5 minutes total)
1. **Inciting:** the wrong thing happens, and the hero chooses to stay or act. *Ends on:* the first proof that the danger is real and personal.
2. **Investigation and false hope:** a plan, an ally, a clue, and then the turn. *Ends on:* the betrayal or the worst discovery.
3. **Confrontation and truth:** the hidden truth comes out, and the hero escapes or falls. *Ends on:* the final irony or sting.

### 4 parts
1. Inciting
2. Digging deeper, and the first escape fails
3. **Midpoint reversal:** what we trusted is the threat, and the hero stops reacting and starts acting
4. Climax, truth, and sting

### 5 parts (default)
1. **Inciting:** the world, the hook, and the first threat
2. **Rules of the danger:** how it works, and the first escape fails
3. **Discovery:** evidence of the hidden truth (a previous victim, a recording) and a new ally
4. **Reversal / all is lost:** the ally betrays the hero or the plan collapses, and the hero is trapped
5. **Climax:** the truth, the final choice, and the sting

### 10 parts (a season)
1. Inciting
2. Rules and the first failure
3. A new ally, and the **B-plot opens** (a second mystery)
4. The first real clue to the hidden truth
5. **Midpoint twist:** the biggest one so far, and the game changes. *Write Part 5 as a second Part 1*, because new viewers arrive here.
6. Counterattack: the hero goes on offense
7. Cost: someone pays a price, and the B-plot escalates
8. **All is lost:** the worst reversal, and the B-plot connects to the A-plot
9. The final plan and confrontation
10. The truth, the resolution, and the Season 2 sting

Give the season **a mini-arc every 3 parts**, so someone who starts at Part 4 or Part 7 still gets a satisfying slice.

### Interactive (vote) series: branch and bottleneck
- Each part ends on a **binary choice where both options are plausible and both cost something**. There's no obvious right answer.
- Plan both branches. They **reconverge at the next planned story event**, which happens either way. Example: whether viewers choose HIDE or SPEND, the analyst finds your name; only the route there differs.
- The next part opens with **the vote %, a line for the losing side** ("Spend voters, the car would have been worse"), and **a quoted top comment**.
- The viewers' choice must visibly shape at least the first 30% of the next part. If it doesn't, the vote feels fake and comments drop.
- The script for Part N+1 is finalized after the vote. The plan holds both versions, and the `script.md` holds a "If option B wins" section.
- Credit commenters in captions ("Founders on the case board").

## 6. Anatomy of one part (target 90s, range 75–105s)

| Time (90s part) | Block | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3s | **Hook** | Stop the scroll. The first line, the first frame, and the on-screen text all say the same thing. |
| 3–12s | **Recap-hook or setup** | Parts 2+: compress the earlier parts into 1–3 sentences that end in a twist. Part 1: the "too good" or "too wrong" setup. |
| 12–20s | **Episode goal** | What the hero wants this part, and the cost if they fail. Show the meter. |
| 20–35s | **Beat 1** | Attempt, then complication. **A small reveal at ~30%** to beat the first drop-off. |
| 35–50s | **Beat 2** | Escalation (rule of three). |
| 50–65s | **Beat 3: false relief** | Something works and the viewer exhales. |
| 65–80s | **Beat 4: the floor drops** | The relief was the trap. The biggest turn so far. |
| 80–90s | **Cliffhanger, then CTA** | Cut on the reveal. The CTA lasts ≤ 6s. |
| optional | **Post-CTA tease** | One line after the CTA that keeps them watching and rewatching ("There's a third way out…"). |

**Rhythm rules**
- New information every **5–8 seconds**, and a turn every **10–15 seconds**.
- Never put two purely descriptive sentences in a row.
- Pay off the previous cliffhanger within the first 20 seconds.
- **Stranger test:** someone who never saw the earlier parts understands the situation within 15 seconds, and no name goes unexplained.
- Option: open with a **flash-forward** (the most dangerous moment of this part), then cut back: "Six hours earlier…"

## 7. Hook library

### Hook rules
- At most **12 words**, or 3.5 seconds spoken.
- At least one **concrete detail**: a time, an amount of money, a number, a place, a name, or a body part.
- Contains a **threat or a contradiction**.
- Starts mid-action. Never open with "Hey guys", "So…", "Part 2 of…", "Let me tell you", or "This is the story of".
- The **second line deepens the hook** rather than explaining it ("I'd moved in just three days ago.").
- Spoken in frame 1: no silent intro, no logo, no fade-in from black.

### First-line formulas
| # | Formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Time + violation | "I woke up to someone banging on my door at 3 a.m." |
| 2 | You already did it | "You just stole $10 million. The hard part starts now." |
| 3 | Too good to be true | "The rent was $600 a month. That should have been my first warning." |
| 4 | Impossible detail | "My neighbor waved at me from his window. We buried him on Sunday." |
| 5 | The rule | "My new job had one rule: never look at the guest in room 9." |
| 6 | Confession | "The last tenant didn't move out. I know, because I found his shoes." |
| 7 | Message from nowhere | "At 3:12 a.m., I got a text from my own number." |
| 8 | Stat shock (engine E) | "One bill weighs a gram. Ten million weighs 220 pounds." |
| 9 | Countdown | "I had 48 hours to return the bag before they found my sister." |
| 10 | Viewer dilemma (engine B) | "Someone offers you $1 million. The catch: you can never go home." |
| 11 | Overheard | "'The last tenant lasted 11 days.' I heard it through the kitchen door." |
| 12 | Flash-forward | "By Thursday, I'd be hiding in a basement next to forty pairs of shoes." |

### Recap-hooks for Part 2 and later
- **[Danger state]. And now [new danger].** "My neighbors are trying to kill me. And now the man in charge knows I've seen it."
- **[Fact]. [Fact]. [Twist].** "Your cousin found out, so you paid him 50 grand to keep quiet. He did. His new chain didn't."
- **Social proof + vote:** "Almost 2 million people watched you do it. And 61% said, hide it."

## 8. Retention toolkit (the middle of the video)

### Turn connectors
Rotate them, and don't use the same one twice in a part:
"That's when…" · "Then I noticed…" · "Here's what you don't know." · "The problem?" · "Except…" · "Meanwhile…" · "Which would have been fine, if…" · "That was my first mistake." · "Nobody said anything." · "What you can't see is…" · "And then it stopped." · "But the worst part wasn't [X]."

### Techniques
- **But/therefore, never "and then."** Each beat is caused by the last one, or it breaks it.
- **Rule of three:** "Another door opened. Then another." Escalate within the triplet.
- **Dramatic irony:** tell the viewer something the hero doesn't know ("Meanwhile, the analyst ran your name."). It keeps them watching *for* the moment it hits.
- **Foreshadowing:** "That should worry you." "That was my first mistake." Each one is a promise, so pay it off in this part or the next.
- **Specificity:** 3:12 a.m., $400, apartment 304, eleven days. Exact details feel true and get remembered.
- **Ordinary menace:** threats delivered politely, and danger hiding in normal things (pizza delivery, a grandma, a lock "being repaired").
- **Withhold the monster:** show the effects (shoes, suitcases, covered meat), never the whole thing.
- **The meter:** a visible score that only moves the wrong way. Update it at least twice per part, and show it on screen.
- **A mid-roll micro-hook at ~50%:** "But the worst part wasn't the meat."
- **Callbacks:** repeat a Part 1 line in a later part with a new meaning.
- **Silence:** drop the music for one beat before the cliffhanger line.
- **A looping ending:** a last line that echoes the first line invites a rewatch.

## 9. Endings

### Cliffhanger types (vary them; never use the same type twice in a row)
| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| Reveal | "It was Daniel." |
| Threat | "Half, or I start talking." |
| Impossible fact | "The problem? I lived on the top floor." |
| Decision (vote) | "Do you pay him, or move the bag tonight?" |
| Arrival | "Footsteps on the basement stairs." / "Headlights." |
| Clock | "I checked the calendar. Tomorrow was Thursday." |
| Dramatic irony | "Dre followed you. And someone followed Dre." |
| Overheard | "How long do you think this one will last?" |

### Rules
- **Cut on the line.** No reaction or summary after it, only the CTA.
- The cliffhanger must **raise the stakes or change the meaning** of what came before. Avoid cheap fake-outs ("It was just the cat"); after one of those, viewers stop believing the series.
- **Pay it off in the first 20 seconds of the next part.**
- The last line is never a summary or a moral.

### CTAs
- **Vote (engine B):** "Comment PAY or MOVE. The majority writes Part 3."
- **Theory (engines A and C):** "Comment who you think wrote the note. Part 3 tells you."
- **Follow:** "Follow so you don't miss Part 4." Keep the spoken CTA to one short sentence; the caption does the rest.
- The CTA goes *after* the cliffhanger, because the cliffhanger is what earns it.

## 10. Line-level writing rules

- **6–9 words per sentence on average**, never more than 15. One action per sentence.
- **~170 words per minute**, so 90s is about 255 words. Stay within **250–290 words**, and check with `tools/vo_stats.py`.
- "I" plus past tense, or "you" plus present tense. Pick one and never switch mid-series.
- Concrete nouns and active verbs. No adverbs. Use "suddenly" at most once per part.
- Dialogue lines are short (≤ 8 words), in quotes, and each character sounds different.
- No exposition dumps: at most two background sentences in a row.
- Write for the ear. Read it aloud, and rewrite anything you stumble on.
- Aim for a grade 4–6 reading level, because a large share of the audience are non-native English speakers.
- For TTS, write numbers the way they should be spoken if the voice misreads them ("three twelve a.m.").

### 10.1 Voiceover format: delivery tags (required)
Every VO is written as **short paragraphs, each opening with a bracketed delivery direction**, so the voice model (e.g. ElevenLabs v3) knows the emotion and pace:

```
[low, tense, almost a whisper] You just fell off a cruise ship. It's 2:14 a.m. Nobody saw.

[flat, cold] And the ship didn't stop.

[quick, factual, building urgency] Deck five is fifty feet above the water. You hit it at almost forty miles an hour.
```

Rules:
- **One tag per paragraph, at the start.** A paragraph is 1–4 sentences with one emotional temperature. When the emotion changes, start a new paragraph with a new tag.
- **A tag is 2–6 words**: emotion + pace/volume (+ emphasis). For example: `[calm, matter-of-fact]`, `[urgent, faster]`, `[slowing down, heavy]`, `[whispering, menacing]`, `[breaking, barely holding it together]`, `[cold, clipped]`.
- **Tags follow the retention map:** hooks are low and intimate; facts are quick and confident; turns are a beat slower; the cliffhanger line is slow, quiet, and isolated in its own paragraph; the CTA is lighter and direct, never cheesy.
- **Give dialogue its own paragraph** with the character's delivery: `[whispering, cold] "Bye, Marcus."`
- **Vary the tags.** The same tag on three paragraphs in a row means the read will sound flat.
- Tags aren't spoken, and `tools/vo_stats.py` ignores them when counting words.

## 11. Visual and sound direction

- **Vertical 9:16, full screen.** Never put a horizontal 16:9 frame in the feed.
- **Burned-in captions,** large and centered, 1–4 words at a time, with key words (numbers, names, the meter) in an accent color. Many people watch muted.
- **First frame:** the hook image, plus "PART N", plus the hook text. Never start on black or a fade-in.
- **Cut every 2–4 seconds,** about one shot per VO line. Visualize every number (a counter, a meter overlay, a map pin).
- **Series identity:** the same character designs, color grade, title-card style, and meter design in every part.
- **Sound:** a low drone bed, a hit or riser on each turn, **silence before the cliffhanger line**, and a sting on the reveal.
- **Narrator:** one consistent voice per series, close-mic, calm with tension underneath.
- **Last frame:** hold on the reveal for about 1 second, then show "PART N+1 TOMORROW".

### 11.1 House look (copied from the reference reels)
Both reference pages use the same look, and it's our default.
- **Drawing:** 2D hand-drawn cartoon with bold clean black ink outlines. Characters are simple stick figures with smooth white egg-shaped heads, small black oval eyes and expressive eyebrows, thin black limbs and white cartoon hands. Each one is told apart by hair, headwear and one signature piece of clothing. In shock, the eyes go wide as big white circles with tiny pupils.
- **Backgrounds:** richly detailed and textured, in muted painterly colors with moody cinematic lighting (lamp glow, neon, moonlight). The detail lives in the world, not in the characters.
- **One accent color for the channel:** saveo uses pink, and we use the yellow (`#FFC72C`) of the main character's windbreaker. Keep yellow for him and for the key word or object on screen. No other character wears it.
- **One permanent main character:** YOU (`channel/characters/you/CHARACTER.md`) stars in every story, and his design and outfit are locked. Only his expressions, poses, surroundings and physical state change.
- **Format:** one still image per shot, a new image every 3–6 seconds, moved in the edit (push-in, pan, shake). Use about 20 images per 90s part.
- **Text cards between images:** cream cards for facts and numbers, dark cards for rules and big lines, a "PART N · YOU VOTED" bar card after each vote, a HUD of stat boxes at the top (saveo's CASH / BANKED / SUSPICION), label chips, and two pill buttons for the vote on the last image.
- **No text in generated images.** Phone screens, notes and signs are generated blank, and the text goes on in the edit.
- **Prompting:** write one complete prompt per image: the shot, the character phrase, the action, the setting and light, then the fixed style tail. Character sheets are generated once per series and attached as references.

## 12. Publishing and series mechanics

- Put the **part number on the first frame and at the start of the caption.**
- **Caption formula:**
  ```
  Part N. [one-line tease that doesn't spoil the twist] 🔒
  [CTA: vote or theory question]
  New here? Start with Part 1 (pinned).
  Follow so you don't miss Part N+1.
  #UniqueSeriesTag #genre #genre #fyp
  ```
- **Pin Part 1** (and the latest part) to the profile grid. Each pinned comment seeds the vote or the theory question.
- **Cadence:** one part every 24 hours while a series runs, and announce the next drop on the last frame.
- **Keep the chain intact:** check that each part is visible to all audiences before moving on (beanie Part 3 was restricted).
- **Make the comments part of the story:** quote real top comments in later parts, credit commenters, and answer theories in replies without confirming them.
- **Track each part:** 3-second hold, average watch time, completion %, follows, and comments. When the hold is weak, change the hook formula. When completion is weak, look for the dead stretch in the beat map.

## 13. Quality gate (every part, before it's final)

- [ ] Hook ≤ 12 words, with a concrete detail plus a threat or contradiction
- [ ] Hook is said and shown in the first frame
- [ ] Stranger test passes within 15s
- [ ] Previous cliffhanger paid off within the first 20s
- [ ] New information every ≤ 8s, a turn every ≤ 15s
- [ ] False relief comes before the final turn
- [ ] Ends worse than it started
- [ ] The last line is a reveal, threat, or decision, and nothing follows it except the CTA (and an optional tease)
- [ ] Loop ledger: ≥ 1 closed, ≥ 1 bigger opened
- [ ] 250–290 VO words (75–105s)
- [ ] Read-aloud test passed

**Score each part 1–5** on hook, curiosity, escalation, and ending. Rewrite any block that scores below 4.

---

## 14. Retention killers

- A slow start: a greeting, a logo, a title card, or "Part 2 of my story about…"
- Explaining the hook instead of deepening it
- Exposition dumps and backstory before the danger
- Answering the series question early
- A cliffhanger that's never paid off, or is waved away in one line
- Every part ending the same way (the same type of cliffhanger)
- Too many names, or new names with no tag ("Daniel, the man in the black coat")
- A vote that doesn't visibly change anything
- Ending on a summary, a moral, or "to be continued"
- A caption that contradicts the episode, or has typos
- A narrator voice, character look, or POV that changes between parts

---

## 15. Responsible storytelling

- Crime and danger stories are **consequence stories**. Use real public facts to show *why things go wrong*. Never give step-by-step how-to.
- Never use real private individuals. Fictionalize anything drawn from real events, and don't defame real businesses or people.
- Keep implied horror implied. Suspense beats gore, and gore restricts distribution (age gating breaks the series chain).

## 16. Horror format (series 003 on, after @zerotales.stories)

The user's model for the horror line ([research](../research/competitor-reels/zerotales.stories.md)). Everything above still applies, with these changes:

| | Suspense series | Horror series |
|---|---|---|
| Length | 87–107s, 250–290 words | **60–80s, 150–190 words**, with `(pause N)` beats of silence |
| Pace | ~170 wpm | ~145 wpm, with fragments of 1–4 words ("Your room. The door.") |
| Opening | Recap-hook, then a vote card with % | **"You chose X."** as the first three words, then the consequence immediately. No vote card. |
| Voices | One narrator | **A cast:** the narrator plus a voice per character (`voices.json`). The monster speaks in a trusted person's voice. |
| Monster | — | A **copy of someone trusted, almost right**. Describe the wrong detail in threes, and keep its lines polite and sweet ("Let's get you inside."). |
| Ending | Varied cliffhanger types | A short reveal or threat, then a **pure binary decision**, with a CTA of ≤ 4s ("Comment LOCK or HIDE.") |
| Look | House look | House look, darker: blue-black night, one warm light, wet highlights. `"accent": "#E3262E"` and `"corner_tag": "PART N"` |
| Caption | Playbook §12 | Emoji headline in caps ("🚪 SOMEONE HAS YOUR VOICE…"), a one-line sting, the question plus the CTA, "🎬 SERIES · PART N", then hashtags |

**Rules of the danger:** give the monster 3 numbered rules, revealed one or two per part by a character who learned them the hard way. Plant the twist in Part 1 inside an innocent explanation ("Her hair's still damp. She must have had a bath.").
